Succinate Dehydrogenase

An enzyme-catalyzed elimination seen through the lens of organic chemistry

The enzyme and where it works

An enzyme is a protein that catalyzes a reaction by binding its starting material (the substrate) in a pocket called the active site, where amino-acid side chains are positioned to stabilize the transition state. Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) is the enzyme in this simulation. It sits in the inner membrane of the mitochondrion, the organelle that makes most of a cell's ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the cell's energy currency).

SDH is the only enzyme that belongs to two central pathways at once. It is a step of the citric acid cycle (also called the tricarboxylic acid, TCA, or Krebs cycle), which oxidizes fuel molecules to CO₂. It is also Complex II of the electron transport chain (ETC), the series of membrane proteins that passes electrons to O₂ and uses the energy to drive ATP synthesis.

The reaction: a dehydrogenation

At cellular pH (about 7.4) both carboxylic acids are deprotonated, so the substrate is the succinate dianion (butanedioate) and the product is fumarate, (E)-butenedioate. Two hydrogens are removed from the adjacent CH₂ groups (C2 and C3) to make a C=C bond. That is an oxidation: the carbon skeleton loses two electrons and two protons.

You can read it as an E2 elimination in which the "leaving group" is a hydride ion (H⁻). A base removes H⁺ from one carbon, the C–H electrons become the new π bond, and the other carbon's H departs with its bonding pair as H⁻. Hydride is a terrible leaving group on its own, so it needs a strong hydride acceptor waiting to receive it. Enter FAD!

FAD: the hydride acceptor

FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme: a small organic molecule bound to the enzyme that does chemistry the amino acids cannot. Its reactive part is a fused tricyclic heterocycle, the isoalloxazine ring (the flavin); the rest (R) is a ribityl chain linked through phosphates to adenosine. In SDH the flavin is also covalently attached to the protein through a histidine.

The flavin's N5=C4a and C10a=N1 bonds form a conjugated, electron-poor system. Hydride adds at N5, and the electron pair is pushed through the ring onto N1, giving the reduced anion FADH⁻. Protonation at N1 then gives FADH₂ (1,5-dihydroflavin): two electrons and two protons gained in all.

Stereochemistry: why only fumarate

Succinate can rotate freely about C2–C3, but the active site grips both carboxylates: Arg399 forms a salt bridge (ion pair plus H-bonds) to one, assisted by an H-bond from His354, and Thr254 H-bonds to the other. Only the extended, anti conformer fits. In that conformer the C2–H pointing at FAD's N5 and the C3–H pointing at the base, Arg286, are anti-periplanar, the same geometry an E2 reaction prefers because it lets the two breaking C–H orbitals become parallel p orbitals in the new π bond. The carboxylates, anti in the bound substrate, end up trans: (E)-fumarate. The Z isomer, maleate, is never formed. Isotope-labeling experiments confirmed the anti (trans) removal of hydrogens.

Inhibitors

Malonate (⁻O₂C–CH₂–CO₂⁻) resembles succinate and binds the same site but cannot be oxidized: a classic competitive, reversible inhibitor. 3-Nitropropionate (3-NPA, the anion of 3-nitropropionic acid), a natural toxin, also mimics succinate, but SDH oxidizes it to a nitroalkene that traps the enzyme covalently. That irreversible, mechanism-based inhibition is shown after the main reaction.

The 3D protein is the crystal structure of E. coli complex II (Protein Data Bank entry 1NEK): SdhA, the flavoprotein (slate); SdhB, the iron–sulfur protein (tan); SdhC and SdhD, the membrane anchor (green and lavender). The active-site side chains and FAD use its coordinates, aligned on the bound oxaloacetate. Residue numbers follow the E. coli enzyme (subunit SdhA). The mitochondrial enzymes of other species use slightly different numbers for the same residues.

Irreversible inhibition: 3-NPA

3-Nitropropionic acid, a natural toxin that shuts down succinate dehydrogenase

Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), also called Complex II, links the citric acid cycle to the electron transport chain. Anything that blocks its active site starves the cell of ATP.

A competitive inhibitor looks enough like succinate to bind the same site; malonate is the classic example for SDH. That binding is reversible: add more succinate and the inhibitor is outcompeted.

3-Nitropropionic acid (3-NPA), present in the cell as 3-nitropropionate, also binds like succinate, with its nitro group standing in for one carboxylate. But SDH then oxidizes it, as it would succinate, to 3-nitroacrylate. That reactive product bonds covalently to the active-site arginine, so the enzyme is permanently disabled. This is irreversible, mechanism-based ("suicide") inhibition.

Where it is found. 3-NPA is made by molds such as Arthrinium and Aspergillus species. It is best known from moldy sugarcane poisoning, which can cause lasting brain damage or death. There is no antidote. It also turns up in other spoiled or poorly stored foods. Some plants (milkvetches, Astragalus, and creeping indigo, Indigofera) and some leaf beetles make it as a natural insecticide, a chemical defense that poisons the insects and grazing animals that eat them.
